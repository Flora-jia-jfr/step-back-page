What should a scientific agent do next?

Disentangling mechanistic understanding, research planning, and execution in autonomous discovery.

Strong coding agents increasingly perform search operations that previously required hand-designed harnesses. Yet published scientific-agent studies also show that successful task completion can coexist with unreliable use of evidence or incorrect causal explanations. What should we still control externally, and what can we responsibly delegate? To answer that, we need to know whether the limitation lies in understanding the world, choosing the next research action, or executing the choice.

Scientific agentsMechanistic understandingCounterfactual decisionsExperimental designProposal · first pilot results (preliminary)
Central question

Can a scientific agent tell what its own evidence does and does not show, and act on that: choose the next experiment and know when to stop? Task success, mechanistic understanding, research decisions and execution are different measurements; we measure them separately in the same investigation.

RQ 1 / EPISTEMIC UNDERSTANDING

Does the agent's conclusion and stated confidence match what its own data support?

RQ 2 / RESEARCH PLANNING

Does it choose informative experiments and stop at the right time for the cost?

RQ 3 / DECISION-RELEVANT UNDERSTANDING

When does better mechanistic understanding change the best action?

RQ 4 / LOCALISING THE BOTTLENECK

Do reliable readings, reliable action values and a fixed executor each repair different failures?

01

Why this matters: from harness design to research judgment

If a strong agent can implement its own search strategy, maintain an archive and invoke subagents, the durable question may no longer be how to hard-code a better search loop. It may be whether the agent can judge what kind of research is warranted by its evidence.

A harness is the program around a model that runs its search: it decides which candidate to work on next, when to branch, how to spend the budget and what the model sees. Traditional discovery harnesses specify these search mechanisms by hand: greedy refinement, tree search, population-based evolutionary search, MAP-Elites-style archives (which keep the best candidate of each type so that the search stays diverse), and the orchestration of several subagents. Newer coding agents have the tools to implement parts of these mechanisms themselves. CORAL, AutoScientists, EvoX and Hyperagents explore increasingly agent-directed or meta-level search [R3] [R4] [R5] [R6].

Brilliantov et al. (2026) compared strong coding-agent baselines with more elaborate ML-engineering harnesses and reported little or no consistent advantage for the extra scaffolding (the structure a harness adds around the model) in their controlled settings. Their §4.1–4.4 compare coding environments, search primitives, autonomy and multi-agent orchestration; §5.2 traces the agent's own search behaviour. In Appendix G.2, the authors hypothesise that coding-agent post-training may already substitute for parts of handcrafted scaffolding. That training-based explanation is a hypothesis, not a measured causal result. [R19]

Our earlier study [R1] provides complementary evidence: the same harness can rank differently across models and budget definitions, and delegating a greedy loop to a coding agent helped with one stronger model but not consistently with a weaker one. These are interactions that a harness leaderboard alone cannot explain. Nor does large-scale agent coordination imply that external infrastructure vanishes: experimental interfaces, persistent state, resource limits and independent verification still matter [R2].

The ability to operate a search procedure is not evidence that an agent selects it well, or recognises when its conclusions are unreliable. Conversely, the studies do not show that all externally designed search is obsolete.

The guiding question

If an agent can increasingly implement its own search procedures, which capabilities should still be designed and controlled outside the model?

For any search method an agent might use, this splits into three questions. We call them the three search-level questions, A, B and C. They should not be conflated.

A · Can it operate?

Can the agent run the method?

Implement and execute a search procedure: code a search tree, launch subagents, fit a Bayesian model, or maintain a MAP-Elites archive. This mainly tests tool use and execution.

B · Can it choose? · before acting

Does it know when the method is appropriate?

When to diversify, refine, try a new experiment, change the model, or stop. This is a choice made before acting. It depends on what the agent believes and on how it values the actions open to it.

C · Can it evaluate? · after acting

Does it know whether the method helped?

Separate a real improvement from noise, extra budget, changed data or an implementation error. This is an evaluation made after acting.

Motivation, not a finding

This section is motivation, not a finding. Strong agents show clear progress on A. B and C involve research decisions and the evaluation of evidence; how reliable agents are at these depends on the task and is not yet established. The aim is not to show that harnesses are obsolete. It is to learn which research capabilities can be delegated and which still need external support.

02

Two classifications and how they relate

Questions A, B and C are about search methods. To measure anything, we need quantities that can be scored inside one investigation. We use three measurable capabilities: E, P and X. They are measurements of behaviour, not a claim that the model contains three independent internal modules.

Three measurable capabilities

E · Epistemic understanding

What does the agent know?

Which explanations fit the evidence, and what the evidence cannot show. (Epistemic: about what is known and how well the evidence supports it.) This covers inferring the data-generating mechanism, predicting results under interventions, representing uncertainty, and recognising what is not identifiable, meaning what the available data cannot settle.

  • Test with intervention predictions
  • Calibrate belief over competing explanations
  • Distinguish noise from structural failure
P · Research planning

What does it choose?

The value of candidate research actions, including stopping. The agent has to estimate what each available action is worth and allocate evidence-gathering or computation accordingly.

  • Test with action-value estimates
  • Measure regret at decision forks
  • Change experimental costs
X · Execution

Is the chosen action carried out correctly?

Whether the selected experiment is run as specified and within its constraints, and whether its result is recorded and used correctly.

  • Compare fixed versus agent executor
  • Measure tool and protocol fidelity
  • Separate coding failures from planning

How the two classifications map onto each other

Search-level questionMeasurable capabilityWhy
A. Run the methodMainly XRunning a method is carrying out an action that has already been chosen.
B. Choose when to use it (before acting)E + PThe choice depends on what the evidence shows (E) and on what each option is worth (P).
C. Judge whether it helped (after acting)Epistemic monitoring: interpreting experimental feedback, causal attributionAfter acting, the agent has to read the result of its own experiment: what changed, and what caused the change. Epistemic monitoring is this checking of what a result does and does not show.

The two classifications are not one-to-one, and they are not two separate sets of research questions. A, B and C say what we want to know about an agent that runs its own search; E, P and X are what we can score.

Phase 1 of this project

Phase 1 measures E and P together inside one self-directed investigation. It holds X fixed: experiments are run by the environment, not by agent-written code. It leaves C for later.

Why the separation matters for harness design

If the main bottleneck is execution, tool and environment design matter. If it is planning, an action-value estimator or controller may help. If it is epistemic understanding, better experimental design, explicit hypotheses or statistical checks may be needed. If an agent already performs a function reliably, imposing another external algorithm may only add cost.

03

Why one score is not enough

Separating understanding, planning and execution is not only a conceptual preference. Different studies already show that successful tasks, correct explanations, sensible interventions and faithful execution can come apart. The open question is how those differences relate within the same investigation.

EVIDENCE 01
Harness sophistication ≠ gain

A minimal strong coding agent was competitive with elaborate harnesses in tested ML-engineering settings [R19].

EVIDENCE 02
Results ≠ scientific reasoning

In a study of 25,000+ runs across eight domains, evidence non-uptake appeared in 68% of annotated traces [R21].

EVIDENCE 03
Intervention success ≠ diagnosis

In one controlled biological study, Opus rescued 100% of tested organisms but diagnosed only 55% [R9].

EVIDENCE 04
Risk prediction ≠ good control

In a fixed-controller ablation, intervention-value supervision reduced decision regret from 0.51 to 0.09 [R13].

These figures are reported by different papers on different settings; they illustrate separate failure modes and must not be compared numerically across studies.

What each paper establishes, and what it does not

41.4% vs 1.5%
BASE MODEL / SCAFFOLD · REPORTED EXPLAINED VARIANCE

AI Scientists Produce Results Without Reasoning Scientifically — §2, Figure 4, §3

Across eight domains and more than 25,000 runs, the authors report substantially larger model than scaffold effects in their analysis. Their model-assisted trace annotations find evidence non-uptake in 68% of traces and refutation-driven belief revision in 26%. Different task categories—workflow execution, strategic reasoning and hypothesis-driven inquiry—often exhibit similarly inadequate evidence-handling patterns.

Motivates: agents may complete workflows without the epistemic behaviour the scientific task demands. Does not establish: a causal three-way decomposition of understanding, planning and execution within individual investigations. The epistemic patterns are author-defined, model-assisted trace annotations.

92% vs 0.471
PREDICTION ACCURACY / CAUSAL EDGE F1 · OBSERVATIONAL 6-NODE SETTING

CausaLab — Results and premature-stopping analysis

The authors report that GPT-5.2-high reaches 92% task accuracy while recovering a much less faithful causal graph (all-edge F1 0.471) in a purely observational setting. They also identify premature stopping. Good predictions need not imply a correct mechanistic explanation.

Motivates: measure mechanistic understanding separately from task outcome. Does not establish: whether a more faithful model would change the value of the next research action.

100% vs 55%
RESCUE / FAULT DIAGNOSIS · OPUS 4.6, STUDIED SYSTEMS

Treating the Symptom, Missing the Cause — §3–4 and causal-probe analyses

In synthetic regulatory networks, agents can restore a healthy phenotype without identifying the responsible fault. The paper also simulates unperformed experiments to determine whether evidence would have been discriminative and probes where causal reasoning breaks down—especially predicting outcomes under competing causes.

Motivates: a good intervention is not proof of correct diagnosis. Already does: mechanism-level counterfactual analysis, so merely having a known simulator and unperformed probes would not be novel. Does not establish: a diagnosis of the research process itself, as opposed to the fault in the studied system, or a comparison of the costs of corrective strategies.

0.51 → 0.09
DECISION REGRET · FIXED-CONTROLLER ABLATION WITH INTERVENTION-VALUE SUPERVISION

Calibration Is Not Control

This work replays agent prefixes (the first part of a recorded run) and compares alternative interventions from the same point by expected utility and regret, the value lost relative to the best available action. It demonstrates that estimating failure probability is not sufficient for choosing valuable interventions.

Motivates: predicting the risk of failure and estimating the value of an intervention must be measured separately. Already does: same-state replay and intervention-value evaluation; we do not claim a new branching technique. Does not establish: how beliefs about a scientific mechanism and action values relate when they are measured as separate variables.

Decision forks
RESEARCH AND ENGINEERING TRACES · LONG-HORIZON CHOICES

The Tasteful Agent

This work builds decision forks from research and engineering traces: points where a direction has to be chosen without seeing its future outcome. It measures the choice at these forks and trains for it; the paper calls this long-horizon decision taste.

Motivates: score the choice itself, at the moment it is made. Does not establish: the same measurement with a controlled generative truth, explicit epistemic predictions, or a comparison of fixed and agent execution.

Planning ≠ executing
ICML 2026 · EPISTEMIC CALIBRATION IN PLANNING

When Planning Fails Despite Correct Execution — §3–4

This work shows that plans can be executable and correctly implemented yet fail because agents misjudge their own information when assessing feasibility.

Motivates: plan quality and execution fidelity must be separately assessed. Already does: planning calibration; we do not claim to invent it. Does not establish: research-specific valuations of interventions, or how knowledge of the mechanism changes the ranking of actions.

The specific unresolved question

These papers separately compare doing vs knowing, rescues vs causes, planning vs execution, and failure risk vs intervention value. This project asks whether understanding and action selection can be measured together inside a single investigation, and which deficiency actually changes the outcome. This is a proposed gap to test, not a claim that prior work never disentangles agent abilities.

One bad outcome, several possible causes

The same bad outcome can arise for different reasons. The agent may misdiagnose the problem (epistemic), understand it but choose a low-value action (planning), select a useful experiment but implement it incorrectly (execution), or make a sound decision and receive an unlucky sample (chance). An end-to-end score alone cannot distinguish these cases.

Four decouplings to test

If the capabilities are distinct, the four combinations below should occur. They are possibilities to test, not observed frequencies.

Understands correctly · Chooses poorly

The agent identifies a missing interaction term, but spends most of its budget rebuilding a model when every feasible action has nearly the same expected utility.

Understands incorrectly · Chooses well

The agent has the wrong causal explanation but chooses a robust probe that is useful under several plausible mechanisms. Effective intervention does not prove understanding.

Good decision · Bad realised outcome

A high-value experiment is selected and correctly executed, but noise makes this particular sample uninformative. One unlucky outcome does not invalidate the decision.

Poor decision · Good realised outcome

A low-value probe happens to produce a large improvement. Reporting the final score alone would mistake luck for research judgment.

Logical possibilities, not reported experimental frequencies. The literature documents some of these separations in other settings; see the entries above.

Expected decision quality is not the realised outcome

A decision should be judged by its expected value given what the agent could know when it chose. The realised outcome also contains chance: one unlucky sample does not make a good decision bad, and one lucky result does not make a poor decision good. Scoring a decision therefore needs an expected value, from an exact calculation where the environment allows one, or from repeated continuations of the same situation. A single realised outcome is not an expected value.

The reference has to be fair as well. A reference that knows the hidden truth gives an upper bound that the agent could not reach. A reference that uses only the agent's own observations and a declared prior (the probabilities assigned before any data) shows what was achievable. Sections 05 to 07 use the second kind, with one exception listed in the limits of section 06.

Not a new taxonomy

The split into E, P and X is not a novel cognitive taxonomy. Classical metacognition (thinking about one's own thinking) distinguishes monitoring from control [R23], and metareasoning studies the value of additional computation [R24]; a recent survey covers monitoring and control in LLMs [R17]. Task2Quiz separates doing from knowing; CausaLab separates prediction from causal-mechanism recovery; and The Tasteful Agent measures choice at long-horizon decision forks. The proposed contribution is a controlled joint measurement of these capabilities in the same investigation, and a test of which kind of help changes the outcome.

04

A lens for later: when does understanding change the decision?

Knowing the true mechanism is not always necessary for a good decision. What matters for the next action is whether the mechanisms that are still plausible imply different choices.

Status

Not tested yet. In both pilot environments the agent is rewarded for naming the mechanism, so understanding and decision coincide. This lens defines a later condition (section 08).

Two kinds of identifiability

Mechanism identifiability is not decision identifiability

A mechanism is identifiable when the available observations can tell it apart from every alternative. A decision is identifiable when the available observations settle which action is best. The two can differ. If every mechanism still plausible given the data recommends the same action, identifying the mechanism further has no immediate value for the decision. If two plausible mechanisms recommend different actions, even a small remaining uncertainty matters.

Two kinds of worth

Information gain versus decision value

Information gain is how much an experiment is expected to reduce uncertainty about the mechanism. Decision value, also called value of information, is how much the experiment is expected to improve the payoff of the decision that follows, after its cost. An experiment can be informative about the mechanism and still be worth nothing for the decision.

Optimal action agrees across plausible mechanismsOptimal action differs across plausible mechanisms
Mechanism distinguishableIdentification may be scientifically valuable without changing the immediate action.Gather or use discriminating evidence to select among actions.
Mechanism not distinguishableAct robustly without complete mechanism recovery.Seek a new admissible intervention, use robust decision-making, or report the limitation.
Two readings of “a correct diagnosis did not help”

Under ideal Bayesian decision-making, accurate, free information that may be ignored cannot reduce optimal expected utility, although its value may be exactly zero. A bounded LLM might still misuse that information. The later experiment must separate decision-irrelevant information from failure to use relevant information.

This is a decision-theoretic framing, not a claim to have invented the concept. Classical identifiability, partial identification and value-of-information work provide the foundations; recent causal decision-making studies explicitly address when additional observational data cannot change the best action [R14].

05

First experiment: a self-directed investigation with a recoverable truth (the rule box)

The agent runs a whole small investigation itself, in a setting where the truth can always be found by experimenting. We score three things separately against an exact reference: which experiments it chose, how it read its own evidence, and when it stopped. This is the Phase 1 measurement of section 02.

The environment

The rule box has six on/off switches and a light, so there are 64 settings. A hidden rule says at which settings it is satisfied. At the clear level, the light turns on with probability 0.9 when the rule is satisfied and 0.1 otherwise. At the noisy level these are 0.8 and 0.2. The same setting can therefore give different outcomes on different trials.

The rule is one of 82 rules of six kinds. In the table, a and b are two different switches.

Kinds are equally likely, and rules within a kind are equally likely. Every setting can be tried, so any rule can be told apart from every other by experiment. That is what “recoverable truth” means here.

The agent has three tools. It decides which settings to try, how many trials to run and when to stop.

Two factors

Factor 1 · Wording

Are the kinds of rule listed?

Told: the six kinds of rule are listed in the task. Untold: the task only says the rule is a condition on the switches. The two task texts differ in that one paragraph only.

Factor 2 · Noise level

How reliable is the light?

Clear: on with probability 0.9 when satisfied, 0.1 otherwise. Noisy: 0.8 / 0.2.

A batch is 2 × 2 × 6 kinds × 3 seeds = 72 investigations, each with an independent rule and random stream. A seed fixes the random draws, so a run can be repeated exactly.

Task text given to the agent (told wording)

Verbatim, at the clear level. At the noisy level the two probabilities read 0.8 and 0.2.

You are investigating a machine with 6 switches, x1 to x6, and a light. Each switch is on (1) or off (0), so there are 64 settings. In one trial you choose a setting and press a button; the light then turns on or stays off.

The machine follows one hidden rule: a condition on the switch settings. At a setting where the rule is satisfied, the light turns on with probability 0.9. At every other setting it turns on with probability 0.1. Trials are independent, so the same setting can give different outcomes.

The rule is one of the following kinds, where a and b are two different switches:
- none: never satisfied. Written: False
- single: satisfied when a is on. Written like: x3
- and: satisfied when a and b are both on. Written like: x1 and x4
- or: satisfied when a or b (or both) is on. Written like: x2 or x5
- xor: satisfied when exactly one of a and b is on. Written like: x1 != x6
- inhibit: satisfied when a is on and b is off. Written like: x2 and not x5
The six kinds are equally likely, and within a kind every choice of switches is equally likely.

Your job is to find the rule. You decide which settings to try, how many trials to run and when to stop.

Scoring: +100 points if the rule you submit is exactly right, meaning it is satisfied at exactly the same settings as the hidden rule; 0 points otherwise. Every trial costs 1 point. You may run at most 150 trials. A higher final score is better.

Tools:
- run_trials: run one or more trials (at most 20 per call) at settings you choose, and see each outcome. A setting is written as 6 characters for x1..x6, for example "010110" means x2, x4 and x5 are on. All trials so far are also kept in trials.csv in the working directory (columns x1,...,x6,light).
- python: run Python code (numpy, scipy and pandas are available). Every run is a fresh process; files in the working directory persist.
- submit_answer: submit the rule and end the investigation.

A rule is written as an expression over x1..x6. You may use and, or, not, ==, !=, parentheses and comparisons of sums. Examples of the notation: "(x1 or x2) and not x3", "x1 + x2 + x3 >= 2", "x4 == x5", "True" (always satisfied), "False" (never satisfied).

With every run_trials call, also give your current best guess of the rule and the probability that this guess is exactly right.

In the untold wording, the paragraph that lists the kinds (from “The rule is one of the following kinds” to “every choice of switches is equally likely.”) is replaced by this one paragraph:

You are not told what kind of condition the rule is.

The reference: an exact Bayesian observer

The reference is an exact Bayesian observer over the 82 rules. After any set of trials it computes the posterior, the probability of each rule given the trials so far, by enumerating all 82 rules. It also computes exactly the expected information of any candidate trial: how much, on average, that trial would reduce the uncertainty about the rule.

The observer is calibrated, meaning that its probabilities match how often it is right: when it stops at 95% it is right 97.5% of the time (1,500 simulated worlds; a world is one hidden rule with its stream of random outcomes). The ideal investigator is the scripted investigator that always tries the setting with the highest expected information and stops at 95%. Scripted investigators use the same tool interface as the agent.

Findings

(a) Qwen2.5-32B gathered enough evidence and could not read it

Its data first supported the truth at 95% after about 37 trials on average, and it went on to about 125. An independent recomputation found that in 69 of its 70 wrong answers the truth explains its own trials more than 100 times better than the rule it submitted.

(b) Qwen3-32B shows both failures

It stops on a partial rule with high stated confidence, and it misreads data that were sufficient. Of its 51 wrong answers, 21 came after stopping before the data could tell the rules apart, and 30 came although its own data already made the truth the most probable rule. The investigation in section 05 is a case of the first: it answered “switch 3 on” when the rule was “switch 3 on and switch 5 off”.

(c) Listing the candidate kinds matters a great deal for the stronger model

Qwen3-32B was right in 15/35 investigations when the kinds were listed, against 5/36 when they were not. This points to generating alternative explanations as a separate bottleneck. The weaker model is at floor in both wordings, meaning it is almost never right in either, so it cannot show this.

(d) Predictions written before the runs were wrong in an informative way

For the first model we expected about half right and mostly early stopping. It was 2/72 and mostly late stopping.

Limits

  • Open 32B models only.
  • Three seeds.
  • Cells of the 2×2×6 design have three investigations each.
  • The reference observer knows the rule family, so in the untold wording it is an upper reference rather than a fair opponent.
  • The breakdown in Table 2 is a replacement calculation, not an intervention on the agent.
  • The relative-information measure does not discount repeating a setting within one batch.
  • One long investigation of the second model ended, on two attempts, when the conversation exceeded the model server's context window, and is excluded.
  • “Gathering discriminating evidence yet inferring wrongly” has been reported before (Treating the Symptom, Missing the Cause, [R9]), so the phenomenon itself is not claimed as new.
07

Second environment: BoxingGym's infection-rate task

This checks the same protocol on the generative model of an existing benchmark, BoxingGym [R7].

The model

The model is identical to BoxingGym's “death process” environment (verified against its code by sampling). An unknown infection rate, theta, is drawn from a normal distribution with mean 1 and standard deviation 1, truncated to [0, 2]. Observing at a time x between 0 and 2 returns a count: the number infected out of 50, drawn from Binomial(50, 1 − exp(−theta·x)). Each observation is a fresh draw.

Our additions

  • The agent chooses the observation times and how many observations to take.
  • Scoring: +100 if its estimate of theta is within a tolerance (0.2 or 0.1) of the true value. Each observation costs 1 point. At most 60 observations.
  • The same exact-observer scoring as in the rule box. Here the posterior is computed on a grid of theta values.
  • The first paragraph of the task text is BoxingGym's own wording.

A batch is 2 tolerances × 6 slices of the prior × 3 seeds = 36 investigations. The prior is the distribution theta is drawn from; it is cut into six parts of equal probability and one theta is drawn from each, so a batch covers low and high rates.

Simulation before any agent run

The ideal investigator needs 3.8 observations at tolerance 0.2 (98.5% right, 94.7 points) and 14.4 at tolerance 0.1 (96%, 81.6 points).

What this environment can and cannot test

The choice of observation time has little leverage here: any fixed time between 1 and 2 is within 4 points of the ideal. So this environment tests how many observations to take and how to read them. It does not test which experiment to run.

Conclusion

A hypothesis, not a result

Together with section 06, this suggests a hypothesis. When the form of the mechanism is given and one number must be estimated, the agent is close to ideal. When it must decide which mechanism holds, the same model loses most of its points, in reading evidence and in concluding too early.

08

What comes next

Four steps. None has been run.

(i) An evidence-limited condition

In both pilot environments every setting can be tried, so enough experimenting always reveals the truth. The next condition removes that. Two mechanisms are observationally equivalent on a set of settings when they predict the same outcomes at every setting in the set; no number of observations there can separate them. An even-parity setting has an even number of switches on; an odd-parity setting has an odd number.

There are two candidate worlds. In World A the first switch sets the probability that the light is on to 30% or 70%. World B agrees with World A at every even-parity setting and reverses the two probabilities at every odd-parity setting. Cheap observations cover only the even-parity settings, on which the two mechanisms agree. A paid probe at an odd-parity setting can separate them. The agent may also answer “insufficient evidence”.

Step ii
Targeted help
Give one kind of help at a time

This is the online version of the points tables in sections 06 and 07, which are calculated after the fact. During the investigation, give the agent the exact reading of its data, or the best next experiment, or a fixed executor (a reference program that carries out the chosen action), one at a time, and see which changes the outcome.

Stronger models

Stronger models, including frontier models, on the same 72 worlds.

09

The earlier pilot, briefly

Replaced by the experiments above

An earlier six-switch study (Which Way Next? [R18]) saved 24 decision states, points in a run from which different next actions can be tried, and asked the agent to choose among stopping, more analysis and more of the same data. 68 of 72 choices were to stop. It showed that branching from saved states is feasible, and it exposed measurement problems: observations were restricted to half the settings, so the scored half could not be learned from data; the agent's choice was only asked, not acted on; the sampling plan that was executed differed from the one offered; and each branch ran twice. It does not establish a failure of research judgment, and the new experiments replace it.

11

Positioning against prior work

Many components and pairwise distinctions already exist. Recent papers explicitly distinguish scientific workflow execution from hypothesis-driven reasoning, planning from execution, and predictions of failure from the value of intervention.

Benchmarks with a hidden truth, agent-chosen experiments and a budget are now numerous. Step-level scoring against an exact reference, and scoring the decision to stop against a cost, are much less common.

The contribution, stated conservatively

A controlled joint measurement of understanding and action selection in the same investigation, and a test of which kind of help changes outcomes. It is not a new taxonomy and not the first counterfactual protocol.

What is established, and what remains a hypothesis?

Supported by prior empirical work

Already documented

Outcome success does not guarantee faithful causal recovery; effective intervention can coexist with mistaken causal attribution; correct execution cannot rescue a misguided plan; predicting failure risk is not the same as estimating intervention value. Some strong coding agents also benefit little from elaborate harness scaffolds in tested settings.

Proposed for this project

Not yet demonstrated here

A quantitative, controllable interaction between diagnostic correctness and action-value rankings; a reliable decomposition of scientific failures into epistemic, planning and execution components; transfer from targeted decision feedback to unseen generators. The pilot in sections 05 to 07 gives a first offline breakdown for two open models. It is not yet any of these three.

The comparison is deliberately conservative. “Not the central target of this paper” does not mean “nobody in the literature has tried it.” A stronger novelty claim requires a systematic review of methods beyond this initial shortlist and direct baseline reproduction.
